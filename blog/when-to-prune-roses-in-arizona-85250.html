When to Prune Roses in Arizona: The Mid-January Window for Scottsdale and Phoenix Gardens

Prune roses in Arizona's low desert from late December through February, with mid-January the classic target. How much to cut, where to cut and when to feed.

When should you prune roses in Arizona? In the Phoenix-area low desert, late December through February is the window, and mid-January is the classic target. The Mesa-East Valley Rose Society, writing for Mesa Community College's rose garden, says pruning earlier risks frost damage. University of Arizona Extension's January reminder says to prune around the middle of the month, cutting canes back one-third to one-half just above outward-facing buds.

What to do in November instead: keep watering, deadhead and remove dead wood, and save the hard cut for January. Here's the full plan for 85250 gardens.

Data note: Guidance comes from the Mesa-East Valley Rose Society's pruning guide (hosted by Mesa Community College), University of Arizona Cooperative Extension publication AZ1305 (Rose Care in the Low Desert), UA Extension's January gardening reminder (written for Tucson) and UA Backyard Gardener #222, read November 9, 2026.

Arizona Rose Pruning at a Glance

  • When: late December–February; mid-January is the classic target
  • Too early: earlier pruning risks frost damage on tender new growth
  • How much: cut canes back 1/3 to 1/2
  • Where: about 1/4 inch above an outward-facing bud eye, angled about 45 degrees
  • Recovery: about 70–85 days for the bush to rebound, per the Rose Society
  • Feed: slow-release fertilizer after pruning, then every six weeks until June (AZ1305)

Why January in the Low Desert?

The Rose Society explains that roses don't truly go dormant in our climate, but they still need a rest period to store the sugars and starches that fuel spring growth. Pruning in that rest window directs the plant's energy into fewer, stronger buds, for longer stems and bigger blooms.

Prune too early and the plant pushes tender growth right into our coldest nights. That's the frost damage risk. For freeze nights after you prune, see our guide on how to protect plants from frost in Scottsdale.

How to Prune, Step by Step

Gather the right tools

The Rose Society's list includes:

  • Leather gloves and arm protection
  • Bypass pruners (anvil blades crush canes)
  • Loppers and a narrow pruning saw for older canes
  • A wire brush to clean the bud union
  • White wood glue to seal cuts on canes 1/4 inch or larger, which keeps out cane borers
  • Alcohol or a bleach solution (1 part bleach to 8 parts water) to clean blades between diseased canes

Make the cuts

  1. Remove dead, diseased and crossing canes first. UA Backyard Gardener notes a live cane has a white center and green inner bark.
  2. Open the center so canes radiate out like an urn or vase.
  3. Cut 1/4 inch above an outward-facing bud eye, angled about 45 degrees.
  4. Remove suckers growing from below the bud union at any time.
  5. Strip remaining leaves and clean debris around the base.

How much to leave, by rose type

  • Hybrid teas and grandifloras: keep 4–8 good canes; remove 1/3 to 1/2 of the bush
  • Floribundas and shrub roses: keep 8–12 canes; remove about 1/3
  • Miniatures: cut back 1/3 to 1/2
  • Climbers: prune to fit the support during the dormant season or after spring bloom; keep 5–6 canes and cut laterals back to 8–10 bud eyes
  • Old garden roses (damask, teas, chinas, bourbons): prune lightly, mainly to shape

After You Prune: Water and Feed

UA Extension's AZ1305 covers the follow-up:

  • Water: keep soil moist 2–3 feet deep. In the cool months, water about once a week or as needed
  • Mulch: 3–4 inches of compost or wood chips around each bush
  • Fertilize: small amounts of slow-release fertilizer after January or February pruning, repeated every six weeks until June. Many rosarians then pause in summer and resume in September, with the last feeding around mid-October
  • Water before and after fertilizing to prevent burn

What to Do With Roses Right Now (November)

  • Deadhead spent blooms by cutting back to the first five-leaflet leaf
  • Remove dead or diseased stubs, which discourages cane borers
  • Keep up weekly deep watering. Our Arizona winter watering schedule helps you set the timer
  • Hold off on the hard cut until late December or January

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I prune roses in Phoenix and Scottsdale?

Late December through February, with mid-January the classic target.

How much should I cut back my roses?

One-third to one-half, just above outward-facing buds. Keep 4–8 canes on hybrid teas and 8–12 on floribundas.

Should I prune roses in November in Arizona?

Only light cleanup, such as deadheading and removing dead wood. Save the main pruning for the winter window to avoid frost damage.

When do I fertilize roses after pruning?

After January or February pruning, then every six weeks until June. Resume in September, with a last feeding around mid-October (AZ1305).
