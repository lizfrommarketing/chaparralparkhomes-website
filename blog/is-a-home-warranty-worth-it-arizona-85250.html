Is a Home Warranty Worth It in Arizona? What 85250 Buyers and Sellers Should Check First

Is a home warranty worth it in Arizona? Sometimes. It's a DIFI-regulated service contract and optional in a resale deal. What 85250 buyers and sellers should check first.

Is a home warranty worth it in Arizona? Sometimes. A "home warranty" is a service contract that pays for repair or replacement of covered systems and appliances, such as air conditioning, plumbing, electrical and appliances, for a set term. It's worth it when the contract actually covers the systems you're worried about, at a total cost (price plus service fees) that beats paying for repairs yourself.

What should you check first? Whether the company holds an Arizona service company permit from the Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions (DIFI), what's excluded (Arizona requires exclusions in bold type), and how pre-existing conditions are treated. In a resale purchase, it's optional and negotiable: the standard contract lets the buyer or seller pay, or lets the buyer decline.

We don't quote plan prices; they vary by company and coverage.

What a Home Warranty Is (and Isn't) in Arizona

Arizona law defines a "home warranty or home protection contract" as a type of service contract (A.R.S. 20-1095). It's sold for a separate price and covers:

  • Service, repair or replacement of structural components, appliances, and electrical, plumbing, heating, cooling or air conditioning systems
  • Or reimbursement for that work

What it isn't:

  • Homeowners insurance. Insurance covers events like fire; a service contract covers breakdowns. See our sister site's guide to homeowners insurance costs
  • A builder or manufacturer warranty. Those come with the product or new home and, as the FTC notes, don't cost extra
  • A seller promise. In the sample resale contract, the seller makes no warranty about the property's condition beyond delivering it in substantially the same condition, broom-clean, with moving damage repaired

Is It Worth It? The FTC's Four Questions

The FTC calls home warranties "really service contracts" and warns they may duplicate coverage you already have, cover only part of a product, or make repairs hard to get. Before paying, ask:

  • Will it save money? Count the upfront price plus deductibles or per-visit service fees
  • What are the limits? Reimbursement caps, accidental damage, excluded systems, cancellation fees
  • How does the claims process work? Slow reimbursement reduces the value
  • What's the company's reputation? Search the company name with "review" or "complaint"

For an 85250 home, I'd add one more: how old are the big systems? A newer AC with a manufacturer warranty may not need a second layer. An older unit is where a plan earns attention. Our guide to how long an AC unit lasts in Arizona helps you place yours on that timeline.

What Arizona Law Requires of Home Warranty Companies

Arizona regulates these contracts more than most buyers realize:

  • Permit required. A service company may not offer or issue a service contract without a DIFI permit (A.R.S. 20-1095.01)
  • Sample contract on request. Providers must give you the terms and conditions before purchase if you ask, or point you to a website with a complete sample
  • Approved forms. Contract forms must be filed with DIFI; selling an unapproved contract is a class 2 misdemeanor (A.R.S. 20-1095.07)
  • Clear disclosures. Contracts must state the price, any deductible, coverage limits and exclusions, with exclusions in bold (A.R.S. 20-1095.06)
  • Pre-existing conditions. The contract must clearly say whether they're covered or excluded
  • Refunds. Cancellation terms must provide at least a pro rata refund, minus benefits paid and an administrative fee capped at $75 or 10% of the price, whichever is less
  • Transfer rules. Any restrictions on transferring the contract must be stated, which matters if you sell during the term

DIFI enforces this. It issued a cease and desist order to LifeTime Home Warranty LLC, which it says was never permitted in Arizona, and recommends checking a company's record on its License Search page before any transaction.

How Home Warranties Work in an 85250 Purchase Contract

In the sample Arizona resale purchase contract (Phoenix REALTORS, Dec. 2024 version), the Home Warranty section lets the parties choose:

  • A Home Warranty Plan ordered by buyer or seller
  • From a named company, not to exceed a stated dollar amount
  • Paid by buyer or seller
  • Or "Buyer declines" the plan

The surrounding contract language says the buyer relies on their own inspection and judgment, and that any broker recommendation of a home warranty company should be independently investigated. Forms are updated periodically, so confirm the language on the version you sign.

For sellers: offering a plan is one way to answer a buyer's questions about older systems. It isn't a substitute for honest disclosure; see our SPDS guide.

For buyers: a plan doesn't replace a home inspection. The inspection tells you what's wrong now; a plan may exclude conditions that already exist.

A Quick Pre-Purchase Checklist

  • Look up the company on DIFI License Search
  • Ask for the full sample contract, not the brochure
  • Read the bold exclusions and the pre-existing condition clause
  • Note the service fee per visit and any coverage caps
  • Check whether pool/spa equipment or other items are add-ons
  • Confirm cancellation and transfer terms

FAQ: Home Warranties in Arizona

Is a home warranty worth it when buying a house in Arizona?

It depends on system age and contract terms. Weigh price plus service fees against limits and exclusions.

Who pays for a home warranty in Arizona, the buyer or the seller?

Either. The standard resale contract lets you choose, set a dollar cap, or decline.

How do I know if an Arizona home warranty company is legitimate?

Check for a DIFI service company permit on DIFI's License Search page.

Can I cancel a home warranty in Arizona and get a refund?

Yes. Arizona requires at least a pro rata refund, minus benefits paid and a fee of no more than $75 or 10%, whichever is less.
