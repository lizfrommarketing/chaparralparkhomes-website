How to Fix a Running Toilet: Flapper, Chain, Fill Valve and the 10-Minute Dye Test for 85250 Homes

Toilet keeps running? Find the cause in minutes with EPA's dye test, then fix the flapper, chain, float or fill valve. Plus Scottsdale Water's leak alerts and the $100 monitor rebate.

How do you fix a running toilet? Turn off the water at the valve behind the toilet, lift the tank lid and look: water leaking into the bowl usually means a worn flapper or a tangled chain, while water spilling into the overflow tube means the float is set too high or the fill valve is failing. Most fixes take under an hour and a part that costs less than a dinner out.

Why bother? The EPA says worn toilet flappers are one of the most common household leaks, and that the average home's leaks waste more than 9,300 gallons a year. In a desert city that prices water in tiers, a silent leak is money down the drain.

Data note: Leak figures, the dye test and flapper guidance are from the EPA's WaterSense Fix a Leak Week and Statistics and Facts pages. Rebate and leak-alert details are from Scottsdale Water's Rebates page. Read November 20, 2026.

First, Confirm the Leak: EPA's Dye Test

  • Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank (not the bowl)
  • Wait 10 minutes without flushing
  • If color appears in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper
  • Flush afterward so the dye doesn't stain the tank

A toilet that refills by itself every so often, or one you can hear hissing at night, is telling you the same thing.

The Four Usual Suspects

1. A worn or warped flapper

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. The EPA says it should be checked periodically and replaced at least every five years, because worn rubber can let a toilet flush on its own or leak thousands of gallons a year without a sound.

Fix: shut off the supply valve, flush to drain the tank, unhook the old flapper from the pegs on the overflow tube and clip on the new one. The EPA suggests bringing the old flapper, or a photo, to the store and noting its width and whether it has a foam float.

2. A chain that's too short, too long or tangled

  • Too short: holds the flapper slightly open
  • Too long: gets trapped under the flapper
  • Fix: leave a little slack with the flapper closed and trim any extra

3. A float set too high

If water keeps trickling into the overflow tube, the tank is filling past its shutoff point.

  • Fix: lower the float (a screw or clip on most modern fill valves) so water stops about an inch below the top of the overflow tube; many tanks also have a fill line marked inside

4. A tired fill valve

If the valve hisses, won't shut off, or the float adjustment doesn't help, the fill valve itself may be worn. Replacement kits are sold at any home store; if you're not comfortable with supply connections, this is a quick visit for a licensed plumber.

Quick Diagnostic Cheat Sheet

  • Bowl fills slowly, tank refills on its own: flapper or chain
  • Water running into the overflow tube: float too high or fill valve
  • Constant hiss, float adjusted correctly: fill valve
  • Flush is weak and the tank never fills: fill valve or a partly closed supply valve

How It Adds Up (EPA WaterSense)

  • More than 9,300 gallons a year wasted by the average household's leaks
  • 9% of homes have leaks that waste 50 gallons or more per day
  • Fixing easy household leaks can save about 10% on water bills
  • A family of four using more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter may have a serious leak, the EPA suggests

Winter is the perfect time to check. With landscape watering way down, a jump on your bill is easier to spot.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I fix a toilet that keeps running?

Check the flapper and chain first, then the float height, then the fill valve.

How do I know if my toilet is leaking?

Do the EPA dye test: food coloring in the tank, wait 10 minutes, look for color in the bowl.

How often should a toilet flapper be replaced?

At least every five years, according to the EPA.

How much water does a running toilet waste?

The EPA says household leaks average more than 9,300 gallons a year, and worn flappers are a leading cause.
