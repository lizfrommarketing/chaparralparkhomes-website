How Are Property Taxes Prorated at Closing in Arizona? A Plain-English Guide for 85250 Buyers and Sellers Closing This Winter

Arizona property taxes are paid in arrears, so at closing the seller usually credits the buyer for unpaid days. How escrow splits the bill, with a worked December example.

How are property taxes prorated at closing in Arizona? Escrow divides the annual tax bill by day and charges each side for the days they own the home. Because Arizona taxes are paid in arrears, the seller usually credits the buyer for days the seller owned the home that haven't been paid yet. If the seller has already paid past the closing date, it flips and the buyer reimburses the seller.

Why it surprises people: buyers see a credit they didn't expect, and sellers see a debit for taxes they thought were covered. Both make sense once you know the calendar each installment pays for.

The Arizona Tax Calendar, in Two Halves

Per Fidelity National Title:

  • First installment pays Jan. 1 up to July 1
  • Second installment pays July 1 up to Jan. 1
  • First half is due Oct. 1 and delinquent after 5 p.m. Nov. 1 of the tax year
  • Second half is due March 1 and delinquent after 5 p.m. May 1 of the following year

So the 2026 tax year's second half, covering July through December 2026, isn't due until March 1, 2027. That gap is what "paid in arrears" means, and it's why prorations exist. (This year the first-half deadline landed on Monday, Nov. 2, because Nov. 1 fell on a Sunday; see when property taxes are due in Maricopa County.)

What the Contract Says

The Phoenix REALTORS® sample purchase contract lists property taxes, rents, HOA fees, irrigation fees and certain other items as prorated, with the proration date typically set at close of escrow. Assessments from a special taxing district are also prorated as of close.

Fidelity's guide sums up the principle: the seller is responsible for taxes up to the closing date, and the buyer for taxes after it.

Worked Example 1: Seller Paid Only the First Half

Say a Chaparral Park home has a hypothetical $7,300 annual tax bill, or $20 a day. The seller paid the first half in October, covering Jan. 1 to July 1. Escrow closes Dec. 15, 2026.

  • Paid through: July 1
  • Seller owned but hasn't paid: July 1 through Dec. 14, or 167 days
  • Seller's charge, buyer's credit: 167 × $20 = $3,340
  • On March 1, 2027: the buyer pays the second-half bill, offset by that credit

Fidelity describes this as the seller reimbursing the buyer for the part of the second half when the seller still owned the home.

Worked Example 2: Seller Paid the Whole Year

Same home, same Dec. 15 closing, but the seller paid both halves in October, so taxes are paid to Jan. 1, 2027.

  • Buyer owns: Dec. 15 through Dec. 31, or 17 days
  • Buyer's charge, seller's credit: 17 × $20 = $340

Escrow officers' day-count conventions can differ by a day, so treat these as illustrations. Your settlement statement shows the exact math.

Closing in 2027? Which Bill Gets Used

Early in a year, the new bill doesn't exist yet. Title companies generally prorate using the latest tax information available, often the prior year's bill, and don't typically re-prorate when the new bill arrives unless the parties agreed to. If a home's value or exemptions changed, ask your escrow officer how they'll handle it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are property taxes prorated at closing in Arizona?

By day. The seller usually credits the buyer for unpaid days before closing.

Why are property taxes prorated at closing?

So each side pays only for the days it owns the home.

What periods do Arizona's two tax installments cover?

January to July, and July to January.

Who pays the second-half property tax after a December closing?

Usually the buyer, helped by the credit received at closing.
